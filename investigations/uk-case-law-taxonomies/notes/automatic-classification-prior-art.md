# Automatic (AI/ML) classification of case law by subject — prior art

*Sub-topic of the UK case law subject-taxonomy research plan. Compiled October 2026.*

## State of the art in one page (and what it implies for Find Case Law)

- **The only flagship public deployment is CanLII/Lexum.** It worked because a hand-built ~42-subject taxonomy was (a) mapped onto historical human classification data (law-society digests), (b) used to train a classifier, and (c) surfaced as a *complementary* layer next to traditional keywords — not as a replacement. Taxonomy stability mattered more than model sophistication.
- **LLMs changed everything in 2023–2025.** CanLII/Lexum are migrating from their in-house Longformer-based classifier to LLMs ("classification is now transitioning to LLM technology"); the Cambridge (Sargeant/Izzidien/Steffek) work shows a general-purpose LLM prompting over full judgment text classifies UK cases at ~87% accuracy against a purpose-built UK taxonomy.
- **The bottleneck everywhere was and is the training/eval data**, not the model: CanLII mined law-society digests; Singapore used its Supreme Court's own catchwords; Austin/Spanish work used expert-annotated sets. A neutral service like Find Case Law has no such digest corpus — so a first UK taxonomy will have to be *constructed* (the Sargeant et al. paper is currently the only published general UK taxonomy of ~108 functional areas, seeded from the Law Society's practice areas).
- **Everything successful is multi-label, shallow and functional** (practice-area level, citizen-legible language), not deep doctrinal hierarchies like West's Key Numbers. Attempts to replicate fine-grained human digests by ML do poorly (31.9% on 279 SCDB categories).
- **Explanability and feedback loops are now standard**: CanLII attaches a feedback link to every AI enrichment; Spanish work builds natural-language explanations from tree models; deployed features survive because humans judge the results adequate, not because of abstract F1.
- **Implication for Find Case Law:** design a small (~40–100) functional, multi-label taxonomy; derive labels with LLM prompting (with sample sets for validation); present subjects *alongside* AI summaries/keywords as enrichment, keep the taxonomy independently versionable, and treat preservation-grade metadata separately from AI-added values (which would sit outside the authoritative record, consistent with the Open Justice Licence framing).

## 1. CanLII / Lexum — the reference public deployment

- **Origin (2021):** Saskatchewan pilot — Law Foundation of Saskatchewan grant; Law Society of Saskatchewan's digest database used as training data; ML-generated classification by practice area shown on CanLII under italicised keywords. [CanLII blog, Jun 2021](https://blog.canlii.org/2021/06/14/new-ai-generated-subject-classification-for-saskatchewan-case-law-is-live/)
- **How it was built (2022):** deep-learning (Longformer — chosen because standard transformers cap at ~500 words; Longformer handles up to 4,000) trained on thousands of cases already classified by the Law Society of Saskatchewan digests and Ontario Reports; Lexum **created a 42-subject taxonomy and mapped the training data's hundreds of topics onto it**; precision/recall optimised; results reviewed by trained legal researchers, not just automated tests. [Lexum blog, Apr 2022](https://lexum.com/en/blog/lexums-approach-to-automatic-classification-of-case-law-from-statistics-to-machine-learning)
- **Known limitations (published honestly):** mimics differing editorial policies per jurisdiction; bias toward topics found in law reports (quality lower for e.g. small-claims cases); poor outside trained jurisdictions; English-only; training-material scarcity. *These map directly to Find Case Law's situation (no digest tradition for most unreported E&W judgments).*
- **Scale-up (2023):** entire English-language Canadian judicial corpus (~3M decisions) processed; subjects shown in snippets with AI keywords and as a "By subject" search filter; French support lagged. [Lexum Part 1, Jul 2023](https://lexum.com/en/blog/automatic-classification-on-canlii-part-1-functionalities); use cases = alert feeds + noteup filters [Lexum Part 2, Sep 2023](https://lexum.com/en/blog/automatic-classification-part-2-use-cases)
- **Interface design (2024–2025):** subjects sit inside an "AI Analysis" bundle with structured summaries (Facts / Procedural History / Legal Issues / Disposition), **representative keywords as a granular index below each subject, and AI "meaningful titles"**, presented beside the decision text in a redesigned page (Mar 2025). Every AI analysis carries a user-feedback link. Each document classified under **one or more of the ~40 predefined areas of law; "initially powered by Lexum's in-house AI model, now transitioning to LLM technology"**. [From AI Summaries to AI Enrichments, Lexum, Mar 2025](https://lexum.com/en/blog/from-ai-summaries-to-ai-enrichments); enablers were Law Foundation grants per province and consortium access to digest data via Law Societies.
- Take-away: rollout was jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction, grant-funded, always additive to existing manual keyword layers, with the taxonomy itself an explicit Lexum artefact (also supported in their Lexbox/Decisia products).

## 2. CourtListener / Free Law Project (US), Harvard LIL

- **No deployed subject classification.** CourtListener's "tags" are **user-created tags** (with public/private tag pages, API); no automatic practice-area/subject tagging for its ~10M opinions. [Tags & Notes help](https://www.courtlistener.com/help/tags-notes/); [Tags API](https://wiki.free.law/c/courtlistener/help/api/rest/v4/case-law)
- **It's on the radar but parked internally:** a 2025 contributor ran "metadata tagging experiments" (ML classification of docket documents by issue) under [issue #5518](https://github.com/freelawproject/courtlistener/issues/5518) — moved to "Someday" in Aug 2025 by mlissner ("we're not big on this project"); topic-filter feature idea recorded in [issue #4429](https://github.com/freelawproject/courtlistener/issues/4429) (requested by API users wanting coverage-by-sector views).
- **Their ML investment went to retrieval, not taxonomy:** semantic/hybrid search over embeddings went live in 2025 ([Semantic Search API, Nov 2025](https://free.law/2025/11/05/semantic-search-api/)); Citegeist relevancy; parenthetical holdings harvested from judges' own text. Free Law Project prefers neutral search surfaces over editorial overlays.
- **Harvard LIL (Caselaw Access Project):** no subject taxonomy of its own; LIL runs teaching/research collaborations (CS109B "Data Science for Case Law": students rebuilt headnote-like summaries with supervised/unsupervised ML on CAP data) and coursework using CAP metadata ([LIL blog 2020](https://lil.law.harvard.edu/blog/2020/05/29/data-science-for-case-law-a-course-collaboration/); [2019 post](https://lil.law.harvard.edu/blog/2019/07/22/guest-post-creating-a-case-recommendation-system-using-gensims-doc2vec/)). Headnote reconstruction is the closest LIL activity — motivated precisely because commercial headnote/topic data is copyrighted (relevant precedent for TNA publishing AI enrichments under an open licence).
- **LawStack:** no evidence of automatic case-law subject classification found. **Third-party hack on CourtListener data** illustrates what one external AI tagger looks like: a Supreme Court tracker using Gemini 2.0 Flash to label ~47 topic tags over CourtListener opinions ([mroswell/supreme-court-decisions](https://github.com/mroswell/supreme-court-decisions)) — i.e. subject tagging is happening *around* CourtListener, not *in* it.

## 3. Westlaw Key Numbers style systems and automation

- **Still human-edited.** West's ~450 topics / 100,000+ key numbers are assigned by attorney editors writing headnotes; the Key Number flow on Westlaw Edge (browse, topic search, filter, hyperlink) remains an editorial product. [Key Number System overview (Colorado Law scholarship)](https://scholar.law.colorado.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2555&context=faculty-articles)
- **Lexis is the algorithmic comparison point:** LexisNexis assigns editor-crafted headnotes to its topic taxonomy **"primarily, although not exclusively, on algorithms"**; a 2017 study compared retrieval relevance of West (human) vs Lexis (algorithmic) classification and found the methodological difference produces measurably different relevance profiles ([Walker, 2017, OSF preprint](https://doi.org/10.31228/osf.io/73qsp)). A 2024 presentation asks whether generative AI will now stand where digests and Key Numbers stood ([OSF preprint](https://doi.org/10.31228/osf.io/bheqw)) but no vendor has published Key Number automation studies.
- **LLM→traditional-taxonomy mapping is mostly commercial/unpublished** (e.g. fine-tuning LLMs against LexisNexis headnote taxonomies is being sold as a service, not published: [Inference Systems](https://inferensys.com/train/generative-ai-for-legal-precedent-and-citation-verification/lexisnexis-integrated-legal-research-model-training/fine-tuning-legal-llms-on-lexisnexis-headnote-and-taxonomy-structures-for-structured-research)). There is even a 2000 patent on digest/classification automation ([WO2000067162A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/WO2000067162A1/en)).
- **Relevant academic contrast:** UK-focused work explicitly rejected importing US taxonomies — Sargeant et al. note SALI is unusable ("bankruptcy" vs UK "insolvency" mean different procedures) and Stanford LIST is US-focused — hence the need for a UK-native scheme (see §4).
- **No public evidence found** that West/Lexis map LLM output onto one another's taxonomies, or that any published study transfers Key Number labels to an open corpus (also constrained by headnote copyright).

## 4. Academic work on classifying judgments into legal areas

Focused scan; eight relevant items, noting approach and any published taxonomy:

1. **Sargeant, Izzidien & Steffek (Cambridge), "Topic classification of case law using a large language model and a new taxonomy for UK law" (Artificial Intelligence and Law, 2025)** — the most directly relevant published work: prompted **Claude 3 Opus** with full text + a predefined taxonomy + self-evaluation; **87.13% accuracy / F1 0.87** on summary-judgment cases; **published a new UK functional taxonomy of 108 areas** (derived from Law Society "Find a Solicitor" practice areas + 44 grounded-theory amendments; six top-level groups: Public, Dispute resolution, Commercial, Personal & consumer, Criminal, International; 3-letter codes like LRG/LRE/PUB). Explicitly motivated by the fact that **UK case law carries no keywords/topic filter** and no standardised UK taxonomy exists. Open access: [DOI 10.1007/s10506-025-09434-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10506-025-09434-0)
2. **Soh, Lim & Chai, "Legal Area Classification: A Comparative Study of Text Classifiers on Singapore Supreme Court Judgments" (2019)** — 6,227 judgments emulating the court's own catchword classification; topic models, word-embedding and language-model classifiers all performed well with only a few hundred training judgments; SOTA methods needed legal-domain optimisation. ([ACL Anthology W19-2208](https://aclanthology.org/W19-2208/))
3. **Sulea et al., "Exploring the Use of Text Classification in the Legal Domain" (2017)** — French Supreme Court (Cour de cassation): 96% F1 for law-area prediction and 98% for ruling prediction with an SVM ensemble trained on full rulings, including masking experiments to simulate case-description input. ([arXiv:1710.09306](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.09306))
4. **De Arriba-Pérez et al., "Explainable multi-label classification of Spanish legal judgments" (2023–2024)** — tree-based (RF/DT/GB) multi-label classifiers over Spanish jurisdictions' law categories (civil, penal, social, etc.), with **natural-language explanations of the decision path, expert-in-the-loop dictionaries, >90% accuracy / 85%+ micro-precision** — the trust/explainability angle a public body would need. ([ScienceDirect S1319157822003664](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1319157822003664); [arXiv:2405.17610](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17610))
5. **Chen et al., "A Comparative Study of Automated Legal Text Classification Using Random Forests and Deep Learning" (IP&M 2022)** — 30,000 US case documents, 50 categories; domain-concept features + Random Forests beat deep-learning baselines; feature-count ablations published. ([PDF](https://haihua0913.github.io/images/paperpdfs/%5B2022%5D%5BInformation%20Processing%20and%20Management%5D%5BA%20Comparative%20Study%20of%20Automated%20Legal%20Text%20Classification%20Using%20Random%20Forests%20and%20Deep%20Learning%20.pdf))
6. **Undavia, Meyers & Ortega (2018), Supreme Court Classifier** — CNN over SCDB categories: 72.4% on 15 broad categories but **31.9% on 279 fine-grained ones** — a caution against ML-automating deep editorial hierarchies. ([Annals of Computer Science proceedings PDF](https://annals-csis.org/proceedings/2018/drp/pdf/227.pdf))
7. **MOSTA (De Martino, Pio & Ceci, Information Sciences 2023)** — unsupervised overlapping clustering of EUR-Lex judgments fusing text + cited-act embeddings, motivated by **label scarcity**; assigns documents to multiple subject-matter clusters without a taxonomy. ([DOI 10.1016/j.ins.2023.118956](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ins.2023.118956))
8. **Locke & Zuccon, "Towards Automatically Classifying Case Law Citation Treatment Using Neural Networks" (ADCS 2019)** — not subject classification, but the neighbouring task (citation treatment/importance) is found to be very hard with neural methods; useful humility data + released test set/embeddings. ([ACM DL](https://dlnext.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3372124.3372128))

Notes on other leads in the brief: **no published AustLII "SIF" automated subject facility could be verified** in this scan (AustLII's documented role is full-text/word-frequency retrieval and hosting human catchwords; the DataLex word-frequency retrieval background is in the [AustLII handbook, ch. 3](https://www2.austlii.edu.au/cal/guides/retrieval/handbook/index-3.html)). Australian value in this scan came from general judgment-classification literature and the Open Australian Legal Corpus ecosystem, not from an AustLII taxonomy. Related UK-adjacent work: a multi-label/deep taxonomy question is also treated by the **UK summary-judgment paper above**, which supersedes topic-modelling precedents (German FCC STM studies, Harvard Historic SC decisions) that it reviews.

## 5. The National Archives / Find Case Law's own enrichment work

- **[nationalarchives/ds-find-caselaw-docs](https://github.com/nationalarchives/ds-find-caselaw-docs)** — the service's hub repo lists all components, including the two enrichment projects below, plus UI/editor API/ingester microservices.
- **[ds-caselaw-data-enrichment-service](https://github.com/nationalarchives/ds-caselaw-data-enrichment-service)** ("Judgment Enrichment Pipeline", MIT-licensed, Python/Docker/Terraform): *"Marks up judgments in Find Case Law with references to other cases and legislation."* It is a three-annotator Lambda pipeline (Case Law Annotator → **Legislation Annotator** → Abbreviation Annotator). The Legislation Annotator finds UK primary-legislation references using **"a hybrid of deterministic and probabilistic strategies"** with fuzzy candidate-title lookup ([docs](https://github.com/nationalarchives/ds-caselaw-data-enrichment-service/blob/main/docs/legislation/legislation-annotator.md)). So TNA already runs ML/annotation-style enrichment in production — **but only for citation/reference identification, not subject classification**. There was an early ontology/RFC discussion ("Feedback on ontology" [issue #33](https://github.com/nationalarchives/ds-caselaw-data-enrichment-service/issues/33), 2022).
- **[ds-caselaw-semantic-shift](https://github.com/nationalarchives/ds-caselaw-semantic-shift)** — research repo: trains **Word2Vec and fine-tuned BERT** on three aligned corpora (Find Case Law judgments, British National Corpus, legislation.gov.uk) to map the semantic shift between legal language and everyday English; ships a prototype "legal did-you-mean" app suggesting alternative search terms. This is a **translation-of-language** project (making case law searchable in lay terms), not a subject taxonomy, but it is TNA's first step toward user-facing AI search support and shows their appetite for public-friendly enhancements.
- **Regulatory/technical constraints:** Find Case Law's stated framing is *"You need to apply for a licence to do computational analysis of Find Case Law records. Other use is licenced under the Open Justice Licence"*; reading/downloading/sharing judgments needs no licence, commercial or computational bulk use needs a licence ([ds-find-caselaw-docs README](https://github.com/nationalarchives/ds-find-caselaw-docs); [Find Case Law about page](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/)). Any subject-tagging project would be a computational-analysis use and should be positioned as TNA's own processed enrichment layer, with the Open Justice Licence continuing to cover the underlying judgments. Judgments arrive as docx → LegalDocML (tna-judgments-parser → MarkLogic), so a taxonomy layer would ride on the existing enrichment/annotation pipeline architecture (privileged API for annotating services, MarkLogic backing store).

## 6. UK public-facing services tagging case law — brief

- **Advocate** ([weareadvocate.org.uk](https://weareadvocate.org.uk/)) — the Bar's national pro bono charity. **Not a case-law tagging service**: it match-makes individuals who can't afford barristers with 5,000 volunteer barristers, organising work by \~50+ **areas of law** for intake/matching purposes (their signposting guide maps law areas to eligibility). It's a *consumer-side practice-area vocabulary in action* — useful evidence that functional UK law-area language is already used by the public — but it does not publish tagged judgments.
- **No UK public/free service currently provides subject-classified case law.** BAILII/Find Case Law expose no subject filter; catchwords exist only in law-report–published judgments (ICLR/Lexis-style), which the Sargeant et al. paper also treats as the gap its UK taxonomy fills. This remains open ground — Find Case Law would be first.
