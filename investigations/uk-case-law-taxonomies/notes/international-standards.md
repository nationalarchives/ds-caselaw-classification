# International/scaffolding standards for classifying case law by subject

Research topic: formal standards and EU/council-of-Europe practice for subject-classifying case law, to inform a possible subject taxonomy for Find Case Law (TNA).

## Summary

- **EuroVoc** is the EU's official multilingual thesaurus (21 domains, 127 micro-thesauri, ~7,000 preferred terms, 24 languages, SKOS-published) and *is* applied to case law: EUR-Lex indexes CJEU judgments with EuroVoc descriptors and "subject matter of the case", and since late 2026 uses a dedicated CJEU-maintained **case-law subject matter authority table** aligned with InfoCuria.
- **ECLI metadata** (Council Conclusions of 2011, updated/extended in 2019) defines an optional Dublin Core `dcterms:subject` field with a small **fixed list of law branches** (civil, commercial, family, insolvency, PIL, criminal, EU, administrative, tax, international public, constitutional, public accounting law) — the closest thing to an *official recommended class list for judgments* in Europe.
- **CURIA/InfoCuria** classifies CJEU cases by **case type** (procedural type: preliminary ruling, annulment, appeal…) and a CJEU-maintained **subject matter** hierarchy based on EU treaty competences.
- **HUDOC (ECtHR)** demonstrates the strongest working example: a court publishing with a controlled, publicly documented **keyword thesaurus** organised article-by-article, plus Convention articles themselves as classification facets.
- **Library of Congress Class K** is a bibliographic shelf-classification for law *collections* (general/comparative law, jurisdictional subclasses K–KZ), far too deep and book-oriented for public case-law faceting; LOC Subject Headings are the more usable borrowing point.
- Overall: for a UK public-facing taxonomy, the pragmatic scaffolding is (a) ECLI's law-branch list as the top level, (b) EuroVoc as a ready-made, multilingual, SKOS published vocabulary for enrichment/mapping, and (c) HUDOC as the model for a curated keyword layer. No formal international standard dictates a *court-judgment subject taxonomy*; EuroVoc comes closest to being mandatory-infrastructure in an EU context only.

## 1. EuroVoc (Publications Office of the EU)

**What it is.** Multilingual, multidisciplinary thesaurus maintained by the EU Publications Office; published as SKOS linked data (CELLAR / EU Vocabularies website, ShowVoc / VocBench editorial platform). ~7,000+ preferred terms per language, 678,000 total terms across 24 languages, 8 hierarchical levels, updated 3–4 times a year; governed by an interinstitutional committee ([LDK paper 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.ldk-1.34.pdf), [EU Vocabularies](https://op.europa.eu/en/web/eu-vocabularies)).

**Structure relevant to law.**
- 21 top-level domains ("fields"), 127 micro-thesauri. Law-relevant domains (legacy two-digit numbering, stable across languages): **12 LAW** (micro-thesauri: sources and branches of the law; civil law; criminal law; justice; organisation of the legal system; international law; rights and freedoms), **10 EUROPEAN UNION** (incl. *European Union law*), **24 FINANCE** (incl. taxation, insurance), **20 TRADE**, **28 SOCIAL QUESTIONS**, **04 POLITICS**, **08 INTERNATIONAL RELATIONS**, **16 ECONOMICS**, **44 EMPLOYMENT AND WORKING CONDITIONS**, **40 BUSINESS AND COMPETITION**, etc. ([EuroVoc thesaurus documentation](http://publications.europa.eu/resource/cellar/7eecbd11-c00d-11e5-9e54-01aa75ed71a1.0002.01/DOC_1); [EUR-Lex Browse by EuroVoc](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/browse/eurovoc.html)).
- In the modern SKOS "EuroVoc domains" scheme the same structure carries URIs like `http://eurovoc.europa.eu/domains` (see [ONKI mirror](https://light.onki.fi/eurovoc/en/index/)); LAW = concept `1004` in some alignments (e.g. IATE's secondary LAW branch "14 LAW", which **integrates the CJEU's own classification** — see next bullet).

**Use for court judgments — confirmed.**
- EUR-Lex assigns EuroVoc descriptors ("subject matter" / "EuroVoc terms") to every document, explicitly including **case-law and national law of Member States**; documents get one or more descriptors, with ancestors/descendants avoided ([LDK paper, §3.1](https://aclanthology.org/2025.ldk-1.34.pdf)).
- From **October 2026**, EUR-Lex no longer categorises CJEU case law with general EuroVoc subject-matter terms: judgments, orders and opinions are categorised using concepts from a dedicated **case-law subject matter authority table maintained by the CJEU**, "fully aligned with their subject matter classification", terms structured as a hierarchy and related to the treaty legal basis of EU competences ([EUR-Lex developments: October 2026](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/content/development/index.html)). Terms are also aligned with those used on **InfoCuria**.
- IATE (EU terminology DB) uses EuroVoc as domain classification and embeds a secondary CJEU-aligned LAW branch ([LDK paper, §3.3](https://aclanthology.org/2025.ldk-1.34.pdf)).
- Domain-level browsing is a first-class public feature: [Browse by EuroVoc](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/browse/eurovoc.html), [help page](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/content/help/search/browse-by-eurovoc.html).

**Assessment for a UK case law taxonomy.**
- Strengths: ready-made, free, SKOS/linked-data, 24 languages, SYN (non-preferred terms) for search recall; legally literate (legal branches exist as first-class concepts); used at national level by EU Member State parliaments/courts too.
- Weaknesses: EU-policy-shaped, not UK-shaped (finite concepts like `British National (Overseas)` at geo level but branch concepts like insolvency/family are thin in **12 civil law**); the CJEU chose *its own hierarchy* rather than relying on EuroVoc's LAW branch — evidence that even EuroVoc is not judged sufficient for court case law; descriptors index themes (subject matter) rather than procedure regularly mattering to users.
- Verdict: excellent **scaffold and mapping target** (UK subjects ↔ EuroVoc), risky as the public-facing user-facing list by itself.

## 2. EUR-Lex / CURIA (CJEU) practice

- **EUR-Lex case-law collection**: judgments/orders/opinions of the Court, General Court and (former) Civil Service Tribunal are classified by subject in the **Directory of case-law** — since the Lisbon Treaty a scheme of **9 main domains and subdomains** ("The legal order of the EU", "Institutional framework", "Legal proceedings", "Internal policy", "External policy", "Staff cases"…) and a pre-2010 scheme of 8 domains ([Directory of case-law](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/browse/directories/new-case-law.html)).
- **Case type** is separately present as a small closed procedural list, surfaced in any case view: judgment, order, opinion, preliminary ruling (reference from national court), action for annulment, infringement, staff case, interlocutory/appeal etc. The judgment's own catchwords ("(Reference for a preliminary ruling – … )") make procedural type explicit in the document itself.
- **InfoCuria (curia.europa.eu)** — the Court's own search — exposes facets including case type ("type of proceedings"), subject matter, filing/published dates, referring court, keywords in the JURI database; each judgment record carries CJEU-assigned **"Subject matter"** and keyword headnote data. Direct scraping is awkward (JSF UI), but the alignment is documented: EUR-Lex states its case-law subject matter terms "are aligned with those used to classify case-law on InfoCuria", from a "**case-law subject matter authority table** … maintained by the Court of Justice" ([EUR-Lex developments, Oct 2026](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/content/development/index.html)).
- **Assessment.** The CJEU's model = procedural type (small closed list) + subject matter hierarchy tied to treaty competences + headnote keywords. For Find Case Law, the "procedural type vs substantive subject" separation is a directly transferable design pattern; the CJEU's subject hierarchy, however, is an EU-competence map, not a general law map, and its public browsing experience is weak (JSF faceted search, no browsable taxonomy page of publishable quality).

## 3. ECLI (European Case Law Identifier) & European e-Justice

- **Identifier**: 5 mandatory elements `ECLI:<country>:<court>:<year>:<number>` ([e-Justice ECLI page](https://e-justice.europa.eu/topics/legislation-and-case-law/european-case-law-identifier-ecli_en)).
- **Metadata** defined by Council Conclusions inviting voluntary adoption, expressed in **Dublin Core terms**:
  - Mandatory (2011): identifier, isVersionOf (the ECLI), creator (court), coverage (country), date, language, publisher, accessRights, type ([2011 Conclusions](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A52011XG0429%2801%29)).
  - Optional but central to this research: **`dcterms:subject`** — "used to indicate the field of law. It should contain one or more items from a scheme containing values for **civil law, commercial law, family law, insolvency law, private international law, criminal law, EU law, administrative law, tax law, international public law and constitutional law**, and may contain a more specific description of the field of law"; plus `dcterms:description` ("keywords or headnotes") — i.e. **only subject + description are open-keyword slots**.
  - The **2019 update** ([Council Conclusions of 24.10.2019](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=oj%3AJOC_2019_360_R_0001) / [OJ PDF](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=OJ:C:2019:360:FULL&from=EN)) extends the work-level model: Subject becomes a `0:n`, multilingual field whose **fixed values** are the 11 branches above **plus "public accounting law"**, additional values allowed; also adds neutral citation, importance level (low/medium/high), judge/party contributors, references (ECLI/CELEX/ELI/AKN).
- **Uptake**: national ECLI implementations across most EU Member States + Lugano states; metadata surfaced via the e-Justice ECLI resolver (`e-justice.europa.eu/ecli/`). The `subject` field is optional, so coverage of law-branch values is patchy across national providers.
- **Assessment.** The 12-branch fixed list is the most *normative*, multilingual-neutral, deliberately simple law-branch list in Europe. It is too coarse to be a whole taxonomy, but it is well suited as the **top tier of a UK taxonomy** (UK does not have most EU-law framing; map "EU law" accordingly). The explicit two-layer design (fixed branches + free additional descriptors) is the pattern an official public service can adopt. UK courts currently sit outside ECLI practice (UK law reports aren't ECLI-ed), but the scheme's vocabulary choices are jurisdiction-agnostic for civil/administrative/criminal etc.

## 4. HUDOC (ECtHR) — the strongest working example

- The ECtHR publishes each case with classification by: **Convention articles/protocols** engaged (facets), and **keywords chosen from a curated thesaurus** ("terms taken (in most cases) directly from the text of the Convention and additional Protocols"), published as a public article-by-article list article by article of article-level keywords ([HUDOC keywords list](https://www.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr/hudoc_keywords_eng), [HUDOC database page](https://www.echr.coe.int/hudoc-database)).
- Examples of granularity: Article-specific keywords with paragraph sub-indexing (e.g. Article 8 → keywords under "Paragraph 2"; Article 4-1 → "Slavery", "Servitude", "Trafficking in human beings"); dozens of Article 6 keywords. Plus a small set of **"keywords of general application"**: *Positive obligations, Proportionality, Margin of appreciation, Foreseeability, Effective investigation, Safeguards against abuse, Accessibility, Non-derogable rights and freedoms, Jurisdiction of the Court*.
- Maintained by the Court's Registry/Legal Information Team; the keyword list is **multilingual** — supplied in EN/FR and translated versions (e.g. ES, RO, RU, TR, UK and others) ([HUDOC database](https://www.echr.coe.int/hudoc-database)). The Court Library also uses formalised subject keywords in bibliographic practice with coded patterns (e.g. `ECHR-8`, `ECHRCOURT-procedure`) ([ECtHR Library Search Guide](https://www.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr/library_searchguide_eng)).
- **Assessment.** HUDOC is the best **public, court-run, controlled-vocabulary** precedent: small, curated, structured around an enumeration of legal rules that consumers already know (Convention articles) rather than abstract law-branch classes. For a UK analog, that suggests keying a keyword layer to the source of rights/cases (e.g. statutory ground *or* key statute/measure) alongside a broader subject list. Its weakness: coverage is human-curated, slow, unevenly applied — semantic and automatic taggers could ease the burden ([Mumford et al., JURIX 2024](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3186206/1/JURIX_2024___ECHR_Judgment_Paper.pdf)) but it remains a tractable, high-control model.

## 5. Library of Congress — Class K and related vocab

- **LCC Class K** is a bibliographic shelf-classification for **legal collections**, not judgments: subclass K = general/comparative/jurisprudential works, enumerated to deep numeric bands (civil law K625–968; commercial law K1000–1395; criminal procedure K5401–5570; …). Jurisdiction then re-appears through the fully **parallel subclasses KD/KE/KF/KJ–KKZ/KL–KWX/KZ** etc. (UK/Ireland; Canada; USA; Europe; Asia/Africa; law of nations) ([LCC outline, Class K](https://www.loc.gov/catdir/cpso/lcco/lcco_k.pdf)).
- Useful wrinkle: LCC's comparative-law bands classify by *branch* independent of jurisdiction — the branch list itself approximates a law-domain taxonomy (K670 Domestic relations, K623–968 Civil law, K1000–1395 Commercial law, K1401–1578 IP, K3154–3370 Constitutional, K3400–3431 Administrative, K5000–5582 Criminal, K7000–7720 Conflict of laws).
- **Related vocab**: LC Subject Headings have well-developed law subdivisions (e.g. "Law reports, digests, etc.", branch-level headings); the "Classification for the Contents of Law Journals" tradition is a bibliographic fine-classification of *journal types*, not case subjects. Both are naming sources only.
- **Assessment.** LCC is far too deep (thousands of bands) and book/journal-shaped for public case-law faceting; but its **branch layer** is easy to borrow topic names from, and its jurisdiction-then-branch pattern mirrors UK practice. Use as a *source of conventional English branch terminology*, not a deployed scheme.

## 6. Other formal bits (brief)

- **Dublin Core Metadata Initiative (DCMI)**: not a taxonomy itself; supplies the property (`dcterms:subject`) that ECLI uses, so "subject" is conventionally a *scheme + free text* slot with no imposed list ([e-Justice ECLI page](https://e-justice.europa.eu/topics/legislation-and-case-law/european-case-law-identifier-ecli_en)).
- **UNBIS Thesaurus / UN instruments**: no dedicated judgment-subject scheme; UNBIS is a general UN vocabulary, not court-specific. Not relevant for a UK service.
- **ECLI extension proposals** ("ECLI-XL", proposed descriptor extensions) exist in the 2019 Conclusions §C as forward room — worth tracking if a UK service wants future-proofing but currently unused at scale ([OJ C 360, 2019](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=oj%3AJOC_2019_360_R_0001)).
- **AKN (Akoma Ntoso)** and **ELI/European Legislation Identifier** provide document/act-level metadata but leave `subject` vocabulary open per jurisdiction — a gap a public case law service can legitimately fill.

## Key take-aways for Find Case Law

1. **None of the big court services publishes an ad-hoc, fully bespoke taxonomy**: CJEU/e-Justice lean on EuroVoc/CJEU-authority-table; ECtHR maintains its own keyword thesaurus; national services lean on ECLI's law-branch list. A curated, small, court-maintained vocabulary **is** the norm, not the exception.
2. **Three-layer design recurs everywhere**: (a) a coarse field-of-law list (ECLI: 12 branches; EuroVoc: domain/micro-thesauri), (b) procedural/case-type facet (CURIA "case type"), (c) keyword/library heading layer (HUDOC keywords; LOC subject headings).
3. **CJEU's own recent move** (October 2026 change on EUR-Lex) to a dedicated case-law subject matter authority table shows even the pioneer of "EuroVoc for cases" rejecting *general* EuroVoc subject terms as a case-law search vocabulary in favour of a **competence-aligned hierarchy** — i.e. a court-specific vocabulary is worth maintaining even when a general one exists ([EUR-Lex developments Oct 2026](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/content/development/index.html)).
4. For TNA: a UK taxonomy should take the ECLI 12-branch list as the top tier, borrow conventional branch terminology from LCC/LOC-SH and EuroVoc for multilingual/machine-readable mappings, and model a HUDOC-style curated keyword layer (perhaps grounded in e.g. statute/regulation names for niche areas) for the layer users actually browse/search.
