# Investigation summary: UK case law subject taxonomies

## Objectives

Find Case Law (The National Archives) does not yet offer a subject taxonomy. Before designing one, we want a survey of:

1. Existing or past subject classification schemes used by individual UK courts and tribunals when publishing their decisions (e.g. the former Lands Chamber decisions site at landschamber.decisions.tribunals.gov.uk).
2. Schemes used by services which support the public and non-legal professionals in finding case law or understanding their legal problem (e.g. Citizens Advice, Shelter, Advicenow, GOV.UK).
3. Wider context worth borrowing from: other legal information institutes (CanLII, AustLII, BAILII), commercial publishers (Westlaw, Halsbury's), and formal standards (EuroVoc, Library of Congress law classes).

Goals: understand what schemes already exist, who used them and why (self-classification? publishing filter? triage?), how well they map to each other, and what lessons apply to a free public case law API + website.

End output emphasis (agreed with user): applied assessment — each scheme assessed for usefulness as a basis for the Find Case Law taxonomy (coverage, depth, lay vs professional language), not just description. Topics 4 (commercial taxonomies) and 7 (Find Case Law user research) dropped; topics 1 and 2 prioritised.

## Known starting points (from initial light search)

- Find Case Law currently offers search + browse by court/tribunal only; no subject facet. Coverage pages per court/tribunal describe jurisdictions in prose ([courts-and-tribunals listing](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/courts-and-tribunals)). TNA design docs live in [ds-find-caselaw-docs](https://github.com/nationalarchives/ds-find-caselaw-docs) and there is a [data-enrichment-service](https://github.com/nationalarchives/ds-caselaw-data-enrichment-service) repo (currently tags references to legal documents, not subjects).
- The old Lands Chamber site landschamber.decisions.tribunals.gov.uk now redirects (merged into HMCTS in 2011, per [judiciary.uk](https://www.judiciary.uk/courts-and-tribunals/tribunals/upper-tribunal/upper-tribunal-lands-chamber/)); the archived site reportedly offered classified lists of decisions. UK Government Web Archive likely holds snapshots — a promising primary source.
- The Lands Chamber's own description of its work ([The Work of the Upper Tribunal Lands Chamber](https://www.judiciary.uk/courts-and-tribunals/tribunals/upper-tribunal/upper-tribunal-lands-chamber/the-work-of-the-upper-tribunal-lands-chamber/)) is effectively a de facto subject headlist (appeals, business rates, compensation, restrictive covenants, taxation, land registration, mobile homes, etc.) that maps to Find Case Law's prose coverage description.
- Citizens Advice organises advice into a small set of top categories (Debt and money; Housing; Employment; Family; Consumer; Immigration; Law and courts; Benefits; Health/discrimination) with a deep page tree beneath ([citizensadvice.org.uk](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/)). Their site search logs millions of real public queries — potentially valuable evidence of lay vocabulary.
- CanLII uses ~40 predefined "areas of law" tags, now applied by LLM classification ([Lexum blog](https://lexum.com/en/blog/from-ai-summaries-to-ai-enrichments)); a directly comparable modern precedent.
- AustLII published a [Subject Index Framework for legislation](https://www.austlii.edu.au/techlib/standards/siflegis.html) (two-tier, deliberately general-coverage) and WorldLII has subject categories.
- Future items: BAILII's own organisation (by court/database not subject, but check), Westlaw UK topics / Key Numbers, Halsbury's Laws subject list, EuroVoc (used by CURIA/EUR-Lex), plus court-specific historic schemes (e.g. EAT, First-tier Tribunal jurisdictions, Special Educational Needs and Disability Tribunal decision headings).

## Key findings (updated as work proceeds)

The plan was executed in one pass; per-topic detail and full source citations are in the linked notes pages.

- **The Find Case Law court directory has been mapped to official subject descriptions** ([UK courts and subject coverage](notes/uk-courts-and-subject-coverage.md)): the page records 20 court/court-division entries and separately mentions aggregate Upper Tribunals and the EAT. Of the court entries, the UK Supreme Court is the one with a court-run subject extraction already indexed; the linked tribunal appendix points to the EAT, Upper Tribunal chambers and Land Registration Division extractions. GOV.UK “What we do” descriptions supply useful candidate subject groupings for many other courts, but are not controlled taxonomies by default.
- **Dormant court-specific schemes exist and are rich** ([courts-tribunals-schemes.md](notes/courts-tribunals-schemes.md)). The Lands Chamber decisions site is still live (frozen Aug 2025, pointing to Find Case Law) with a complete two-level taxonomy: 17 categories / ~140 subcategories (Compensation, Rating, Restrictive Covenants, Leasehold Enfranchisement, Housing, Park Homes…). The EAT search site has a 43-topic flat dropdown including explicitly marked "obsolete topics" (a record of vocabulary curation); its GOV.UK successor meanwhile carries a **two-level** Category/Sub-category scheme (45 / 192, see [eat-taxonomy-govuk](taxonomy-extractions/eat-taxonomy-govuk.md)). Successors (tribunalsdecisions.service.gov.uk, judiciary.uk) dropped subject facets entirely — metadata was repeatedly lost in migration. Legacy schemes mix subject + procedure + remedy facets in one list.

### Work log: EAT GOV.UK two-level scheme (Nov 2026)

- Extracted the Category + Sub-category facets of the [GOV.UK Employment appeal tribunal decisions finder](https://www.gov.uk/employment-appeal-tribunal-decisions) from the [content API](https://www.gov.uk/api/content/employment-appeal-tribunal-decisions) → [eat-taxonomy-govuk](taxonomy-extractions/eat-taxonomy-govuk.md): 45 categories, 19 with sub-categories totalling 192 unique values (5 duplicate API rows, all under Practice and Procedure).
- Compared with the legacy flat list ([eat-taxonomy](taxonomy-extractions/eat-taxonomy.md)): same vocabulary carried forward; 41 of 42 legacy topics retained; only *Jurisdiction* removed; four added (Employee/Worker/Self Employed, Gender Reassignment, Marriage and Civil Partnership, Whistleblowing); obsolete-topic markers dropped but the obsolete topics themselves republished; sub-category vocabulary pre-dates 2014 statute changes; hierarchy encoded only as a label prefix. With UT AAT and Land Registration, this is a third instance of the two-level Category/Sub-category pattern in tribunal decision publishing.
- **Public advice services converge on a "life problem area" top level** ([public-facing-services.md](notes/public-facing-services.md)): Benefits, Debt and money, Consumer, Housing, Family, Employment/Work, Immigration, Health, Civil rights, plus "Law and courts" — a consistent ~9–12 bucket lay taxonomy. Each service runs two vocabularies side by side: lay problem-verb labels ("Getting evicted") and professional doctrinal labels (Shelter Legal's "Possession and eviction"). The Legal Aid Agency's statutory civil "Categories of law" is the strongest machine-reusable doctrinal scheme. Caveats: lay schemes omit criminal, commercial, tax and public law almost entirely; court/tribunal-type navigation is venue-first, better as a secondary facet.
- **Among LIIs, CanLII is the strongest direct precedent** ([lii-and-commercial-taxonomies.md](notes/lii-and-commercial-taxonomies.md)): 44 predefined flat "areas of law", automated assignment trained on law-librarian digests, now moving to LLMs, exposed as a subject filter + result tags. BAILII and most LIIs classify by court only; BAILII OpenLaw shows the complement: ~24 curated "leading cases by subject" collections. ULII offers the friendliest lay-facing browse; CourtListener deliberately has none (search-first serves professionals, not lay users).
- **Formal standards offer scaffolding, not a ready UK taxonomy** ([international-standards.md](notes/international-standards.md)): ECLI minimum metadata defines 12 fixed `dcterms:subject` law-branch values plus free descriptors — a ready top tier. EuroVoc is SKOS-published and multilingual, but EUR-Lex recently switched CJEU case law to a dedicated CJEU-maintained subject-matter hierarchy — evidence courts prefer their own vocabulary over a generic thesaurus. HUDOC (ECtHR) is the best example of a court publishing with a controlled keyword thesaurus. LOC K is too deep/bibliographic.
- **The academic/industry consensus: the taxonomy artefact is the gap, not the AI** ([automatic-classification-prior-art.md](notes/automatic-classification-prior-art.md)). Sargeant et al. (AI & Law 2025) got 87% accuracy assigning UK judgments against a predefined 108-area taxonomy with an LLM; CanLII proves end-to-end feasibility at 3M-decision scale; findings converge on multi-label, functional, shallow taxonomies + human validation. TNA's own repos (data-enrichment-service, semantic-shift) already provide the architecture and lay-language groundwork; the Open Justice/computational-analysis licence shapes how AI enrichment can be added.

- **Stanford LIST is the deepest existing lay-facing legal-issue taxonomy** ([stanford-list-taxonomy.md](taxonomy-extractions/stanford-list-taxonomy.md)): 1,301 terms in 20 top-level domains, 4 constituent levels, machine-readable (live `/api/terms` JSON, CSV, crosswalks), maintained by the Stanford Legal Design Lab. Domains are lay problem areas ("Housing", "Money, Debt and Consumer Issues", "Public Benefits", "Family") — the same ~15–20 buckets as Citizens Advice-style advice schemes but pushed down to fine-grained issue statements ("Immigrants' Eligibility for TANF benefits"). Terms have plain-language definitions, so they double as training data for LLM classification. Like the US advice schemes it shares ancestry with, it omits criminal (has a "Crime and Prisons" domain but no commercial/tax/property-litigation law branches as such) and is jurisdiction-bound to US federal+state law. Best use for Find Case Law: vocabulary source + structural precedent (lay domains → deep issue leaves), plus its crosswalk facility for mapping to UK schemes.

- **User-provided draft legislation thesaurus, now in scope as a comparison artefact** ([thesaurus-current.md](taxonomy-extractions/thesaurus-current.md); CSV in `docs/thesaurus_current.csv`): 494 terms in 3 partitions — **Subject** (37 top-level policy areas, 2 levels, e.g. Agriculture and Rural Affairs, Criminal Law, Housing and Planning), **Business** (22 NACE-based sectors, letter+numeric codes recorded) and **Regions** (Crown Dependencies, Overseas Territories, Commonwealth). Structural characteristics vs the surveyed schemes: top level = government policy-area granularity (finer than Citizens Advice life-areas, coarser than LIST's 20 domains); multi-parent relationships recorded in a `Related_term` column (rows duplicated per relation — cross-listing by reference rather than by duplicated node); `AltLabels` supply lay/synonym vocabulary (e.g. *Dogs (Dog Control orders; Microchipping; Dangerous Dogs…)*); ScopeNotes hold in/out-of-scope and review decisions — same vocabulary-curation record as the EAT obsolete-topic list.

- **LAMS CCS v1.6 is a proven, production-used lay scheme — the closest structural cousin of Citizens Advice's life-problem areas** ([lams-ccs-taxonomy.md](taxonomy-extractions/lams-ccs-taxonomy.md); CSV in `docs/CCS_taxonomy_narrow.csv`): 346 terms under 14 top headings (Benefits; Housing and homelessness; Employment; Family and personal; Money and tax; Police and crime; Immigration and nationality; Government, law and rights; Health and social care; Consumer affairs; Education and training; Environment and countryside; Communications and media; Transport, travel and leisure), two to three levels deep. Unlike the web-surveyed advice sites it is a *flat metadata vocabulary* designed for the "Subject" field of Dublin Core metadata — it powered Just Ask's (now Community Legal Advice) "intelligent search" from 2001 and was governed by a user panel including Citizens Advice, Shelter and Advicenow; Shelter extended it into its own housing-specialist scheme. Structure: top tier is lay problem areas (same ~14 buckets as Citizens Advice), but leaves are finer and more doctrinal than any advice site's ("Security of tenure", "Intentional homelessness", "Tenancy agreements"). 16 terms are polyhierarchical (e.g. *Discrimination* under both Employment and Government, law and rights; *Credit* under Consumer affairs and Money and tax) — cross-listing recorded directly in the narrower-term lists. Vs Stanford LIST: much shallower (346 vs 1,301 terms) but UK-specific and covers what LIST omits (criminal, tax, public law). For Find Case Law it is the strongest evidence that a ~14-heading lay top tier with 2–3 levels of depth was workable for real legal-information retrieval on UK content two decades ago.

- **CA CCS labels: a flat, site-navigation-derived complement to LAMS CCS** ([ca-ccs-taxonomy.md](taxonomy-extractions/ca-ccs-taxonomy.md); CSV in `docs/CA_CCS_taxonomy_narrow.csv`): 108 Citizens Advice page labels mapped into 7 CA website areas (Benefits 10; Debt and Money 11; Family 59; Housing 12; Immigration 3; Law and Courts 8; Work 5), flat at two levels. From a research project by Caitlin Wilson based on the citizensadvice.org.uk organisation (no public publication located). Unlike LAMS CCS it is not a controlled vocabulary: granularity mixes categories with very specific page topics ("Blue Badge Scheme", "Islamic mortgage") and even actor labels ("Financial Conduct Authority"), and placements follow site navigation rather than doctrine ("Inheritance tax" under Family; "Mediation" and "Credit checks" under Housing). Overlap check vs LAMS CCS v1.6: only 11 of 108 labels match existing CCS terms — the set is effectively a delta capturing topics the 2001 scheme lacks (Universal credit, Forced Marriage Protection Orders, Economic abuse, Non-binary, Student loans…), i.e. evidence of how far lay advice vocabulary has moved since CCS v1.6.

Across the five topics the consistent picture: no single UK scheme covers all of Find Case Law's corpus; a layered design (broad law branches ← LAA/ECLI style; lay problem areas ← Citizens Advice style; per-tribunal jurisdiction lists ← Lands Chamber style; keywords) would compose the strengths of each, with LLM assignment feasible per CanLII precedent.

## Follow-up: candidate public-facing top level

At the user's request, the next design step has been completed: [Candidate public-facing top-level taxonomy](synthesis/candidate-public-top-level-taxonomy.md) proposes 17 everyday/problem-led headings, maps them back to the priority schemes (Sargeant, Stanford LIST, LAMS CCS, CA CCS and ICLR), and identifies cross-cutting facets and validation questions. This is a proposal for discussion and user testing, not an approved production taxonomy.

A further comparison with the [draft legislation thesaurus](synthesis/legislation-thesaurus-comparison.md) found strong thematic overlap but moderate structural alignment: its roughly 37 Subject areas retain policy distinctions that the 17 public-facing headings deliberately merge. For a unified scheme biased toward legislation, the recommendation is to use the legislation Subject concepts as the canonical shared taxonomy, improve their public labels/aliases, and retain the 17 headings only as optional non-exclusive browse groupings. Keep the NACE Business and Regions partitions, and case-law facets, separate.

A second, legislation-aligned draft of the public top level has since been produced: [Candidate public-facing top-level taxonomy v2](synthesis/candidate-public-top-level-taxonomy-v2.md) rebuilds the headings directly against the [updated thesaurus](taxonomy-extractions/thesaurus-current.md), keeping problem-led wording while naming the corresponding legislation Subject area(s) for each heading and omitting the three Subject areas (Analysis and Information, Economy, Science, Research and Innovation) judged not to arise in case law.

### Work log: v2 ↔ case-law scheme mapping (Nov 2026)

- With the user, agreed a visualisation approach for mapping the v2 headings to the gathered case-law schemes: per-scheme crosswalk tables plus a generated Sankey diagram.
- **First pass**: Sargeant (top level), ICLR, Lands Chamber. Findings: Lands Chamber's 17 categories collapse into *Home, housing and land* (13/17) — mapping works for a court-specific scheme but adds no general signal; Sargeant's "Personal and consumer matters" scatters across 12 headings (residual bucket); ICLR's doctrinal topics split in ways that showcase multi-labelling.
- **Second pass (current)**: per user decision, dropped Lands Chamber from the visualisation (too court-specific), moved Sargeant to **second level** (67 areas — needed because its 6-area top level is too coarse), and added the **UKSC 25-area** list. Also per user decision, **split the visualisation into one page + Sankey per scheme** (the combined Sankey degraded as schemes were added): the hub [Mapping v2 headings to example case-law schemes](synthesis/v2-heading-scheme-mapping.md) links to [Sargeant mapping](synthesis/v2-sargeant-mapping.md), [ICLR mapping](synthesis/v2-iclr-mapping.md) and [UKSC mapping](synthesis/v2-uksc-mapping.md), each with its own crosswalk table and per-scheme Sankey SVG, all script-generated from one mapping file.
- Findings from the second pass: near-total coverage both ways (106 terms → 26 of 28 v2 headings); the two unlinked headings (*Public and personal safety*, *Food and food standards*) are legislation-biased additions with no case-law scheme counterpart — flagged for validation against real judgments; Sargeant level 2 confirms the v2 structure (only four terms need genuine multi-primary treatment); the three schemes triangulate consistently (matching terms map to the same headings); UKSC's composite labels (*Business, property, wills, and trusts*) and matter-type entries (*Veterinary surgeons*, *Mission and Pastoral Measures*) are the stress test, and support keeping subject vs matter-type as separate dimensions.
- **Third pass**: added the curriculum-derived [Legal subject areas](synthesis/v2-legal-subject-areas-mapping.md) (19 areas, doctrinal). It maps cleanly but reaches only 14 of 28 v2 headings — the inverse blind spot of the advice-sector schemes (those miss criminal/commercial/tax; the curriculum misses administrative and life-problem areas), which is the core justification for the problem-led v2 wording. *Your rights and the state (13)* is its workhorse (four curriculum areas route there); the four "Documents" areas are reading lists of instruments, not subjects.
- **Fourth pass**: added the 177 [ICLR Level-1 catchwords](synthesis/v2-iclr-catchwords-mapping.md) — the largest scheme mapped, and the first to reach **all 28 v2 headings**: HEALTH AND SAFETY feeds *Public and personal safety* and FOOD AND DRUGS feeds *Food and food standards*, so the two legislation-biased headings do have (single-catchword) case-law counterparts; real-volume validation remains the test. Around 30 catchwords are courts/procedure/remedies routing to *Courts, police and legal help* — the strongest demonstration that court, procedure and remedy are facets orthogonal to subject. The doctrinal catchwords fan heavily into *Your rights and the state (13)*, echoing the curricula mapping, and the comparison with the 14-topic crosswalk (18 vs 28 headings reached) shows the catchword layer is the better analogue of the v2 taxonomy's lower levels while the Legal Topics parallel its top — a live demonstration of the v2 two-layer design.
- **Fifth pass**: added the [smart survey search topics](synthesis/v2-smart-survey-topics-mapping.md) (`docs/smart_survey_topics_analysis.csv`) — 195 raw, uncontrolled user search terms, the only mapped scheme drawn from real public vocabulary. Method change for this scheme: 21 terms too unclear or tenuous to map were **excluded and listed** (survey noise, meta-topics, unidentifiable abbreviations like *ARAP*, *Gold Clauses*) rather than forced; 172 mapped → 27 of 28 headings. Findings: everyday wording lands on the problem-led headings without forcing (*Renters Rights* → *Home*, *Welfare Benefits* → *Benefits*); variant spellings (*Charity / Charities / Charitable Institutions.*) confirm the alias layer; the survey is the heaviest feeder of *Public and personal safety* and *Government finance and procurement* after ICLR; *Religion* is the only heading with no survey demand; boundary ambiguities echo the earlier schemes; and the empty `COUNT` column means the Sankey is unweighted — frequency-weighting is queued once populated.
